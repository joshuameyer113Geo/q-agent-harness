# 04 — The Cubicle

*October 2024. A sensor operations center. Somewhere the maps don't name.*

---

Her name was Dana Okafor, and she had been staring at the same screen for six hours when the return appeared.

Not appeared exactly. That was the wrong word. It didn't arrive the way objects arrive — it *resolved*, the way a word resolves out of static when you finally stop trying to hear it. One frame it wasn't there. The next frame it was, falling through the lower atmosphere at a descent rate that made no aerodynamic sense for anything with wings, and it was falling the way snow falls. Not tumbling. Not spinning. Just descending in that slow, patient, unhurried way that makes you forget you're watching something move.

Dana had seen a lot of returns in six years of this job. Drones. Weather balloons. A flock of geese that had triggered three agencies before someone laughed. She knew the taxonomy the way a coroner knows the taxonomy of wounds. Fast movers. Slow movers. Hover-then-dash. The categories had names and the names had subcategories and the subcategories had confidence scores attached, and every score was a little prayer that the world would stay boring.

This one didn't fit a category. It fit the space *between* categories, which was worse, because the space between categories is where your training stops and your instincts start, and her instincts were telling her something her training had no word for.

She reached for the phone to call her supervisor. Her hand stopped halfway.

Not because she was afraid. Because she had done this before — not this exact thing, but the shape of it. The shape of holding something in your hands that the world has no shelf for. She had felt it as a kid in Lagos when her grandmother died and the whole house went quiet in a way that wasn't silence, it was *attention*. She had felt it the night her daughter was born and the doctor said *she's here* and Dana had looked at this small furious person and thought: I don't have a frame for this. Not *I don't understand.* I don't have a frame.

The return kept falling. Snow-slow. Unhurried. Like it had all the time in the world and none of the urgency the world was about to demand of it.

Dana's supervisor's name was Reyes. Reyes had been in the community longer than Dana, had the gray at the temples and the flat affect of a man who had learned that excitement was a liability. She called him anyway.

"Reyes."

"Talk to me."

"I've got a return. Descent profile doesn't match anything in the catalog. It's — " she stopped. Started again. "It's falling like snow."

Silence on the line. Not the silence of a man thinking. The silence of a man reaching for a frame that doesn't fit.

"Classify it," Reyes said finally.

"As what?"

"Unresolved."

Dana typed it. U-N-R-E-S-O-L-V-E-D. The cursor blinked after the last letter like it was waiting for her to take it back.

She didn't take it back.

She sat there for a long moment after hanging up, and the screen glowed, and the return fell, and she thought about a kitchen in New Mexico in 1947 — not because she'd read about it that day, but because the feeling had a genealogy. The feeling of standing at the edge of what your mind can hold, looking out a window at nothing, and knowing that something had arrived before the world had a word for it.

Jesse Jr. had stared at that window for the same reason she was staring at this screen. Not because he knew something. Because he *didn't*, and the not-knowing had weight, and weight pulls.

Eighty years. Same wobble. Different glass.

---

*Author's note: This scene does not show the sensor return. It shows the person watching it, and the only thing it asks the reader to believe is that the feeling is real — because the reader has felt it too, at some kitchen table, at some window, at some moment when the world tilted and the nearest frame didn't fit. The foil was never the point. The wobble was always the point.*
